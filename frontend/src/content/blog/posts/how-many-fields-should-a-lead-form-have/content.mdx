A lead form should have as few fields as your first follow-up needs: usually a name, one way to reach the person, and at most one or two questions that change how your team responds. For most small businesses that is three to five fields. Every field beyond that is something a visitor must care enough to fill in, and the visitors who give up are not only the casual ones.

This post covers which fields earn their place, when a longer form is right, and how to ask for more without losing people.

## What fields should a lead form have?

Start with these and add only what your team will use:

| Field | Keep it when | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Name | Almost always | One field, not first and last, unless your system needs them split |
| Phone number | Your team calls or messages leads | Say why you want it: "so we can call you back" |
| Email | Your next step is sending something | A quote, a brochure, a booking confirmation |
| What they need | You sell more than one thing | Use a short list of options rather than an open box |
| When | Urgency changes how fast you follow up | "This week / This month / Just looking" |
| Budget or size | Your offer changes a lot with it | Offer ranges, and make it skippable |
| Message | Rarely required | Useful as optional; a required "message" box stops people |

Ask for phone or email, not both, unless both are genuinely used. A visitor who wants a callback does not need to give an email to get one.

## Do fewer fields always mean more leads?

Usually more submissions, not always better ones. A two-field form gets filled by people who would never buy, and your team spends time finding that out. A longer form puts some people off but tells you more about the ones who finish.

The right length depends on what happens next:

| What happens after the form | A good length |
| --- | --- |
| Someone calls back the same day | Short: name, phone, one question |
| You prepare a quote or proposal | Longer: the details the quote needs |
| A booking or appointment | Name, contact, preferred time |
| A download or a newsletter | Email only |

If your team spends hours on leads that were never a fit, add one qualifying question. If almost nobody fills the form, remove fields. [Chatbot lead qualification questions](/blog/chatbot-lead-qualification-questions/) covers which questions do the most work.

## How do you ask for more without losing people?

- **Use options instead of typing.** A list of choices is faster, especially on a phone, and gives you cleaner answers.
- **Make the optional fields obviously optional,** and keep them at the end.
- **Explain sensitive fields.** "Phone, so we can call you back about your quote" answers the visitor's question before they ask it.
- **Label fields plainly.** "Your phone number", not "Contact information (primary)".
- **Use the right field type.** A phone field brings up the number keypad on a phone, and an email field the @ key.
- **Ask the rest later.** Anything your team can ask on the first call does not need to be on the form.

<Callout title="The one-question test" tone="neutral">
  For every field, ask what your team would do differently with the answer. If the answer is "nothing", remove the field.
</Callout>

## Should a lead form be a pop-up or on the page?

Both work when they appear because the visitor asked for them. A form that opens when someone clicks "Get a quote" or "Book a visit" matches what they wanted. A form that covers the page before they have read anything is the version visitors hate. A good rule: put the form behind your own buttons, at the moments a visitor is ready to act, rather than on a timer.

## Lead form or chatbot?

A form suits visitors who already know what they want and just need to leave their details. A chatbot suits visitors who still have questions. Many sites use both: forms behind "contact" and "quote" buttons, a chatbot for everyone else. [Lead form vs chatbot](/blog/lead-form-vs-chatbot/) compares them in detail.
